In the third week of April 2020, LME cash aluminium traded below **USD 1,500/t**, touching roughly **USD 1,425/t** — the lowest level since 2016 and a fall of about 20% from the January 2020 high. The move was not a supply story. It was the fastest destruction of end-use demand the aluminium market had seen since 2008.

## What happened

Lockdowns across Europe and North America shut car plants, aircraft assembly lines and construction sites within weeks. Aluminium is a first-derivative metal: it is bought to be turned into something, so when the something stops being built, orders stop at the customer's door rather than at the smelter.

Three things followed in sequence:

- **Order books were cancelled, not postponed.** Automotive and aerospace buyers invoked force majeure or simply stopped calling off tonnage already on the water.
- **Inventories rose everywhere.** LME stocks climbed through the spring, and off-warrant and producer stocks rose faster, because metal already cast had nowhere to go.
- **Smelters did not cut fast.** Electrolysis is expensive to stop and expensive to restart. Global primary output fell only modestly in 2020; the production cut that did happen was concentrated in Europe and North America, while Chinese smelters kept running and added capacity.

The result was a price that moved to clear inventory, while physical supply stayed broadly intact.

## What the price did next

The low was short. By the fourth quarter of 2020, LME aluminium was back above **USD 2,000/t** on Chinese infrastructure demand, a recovery in packaging and appliance orders, and a weaker dollar. Buyers who had waited for a "real" bottom in the summer of 2020 paid 30–40% more in the first quarter of 2021.

The lesson that later repeated in 2022 and 2026: **aluminium prices spike on supply and crash on demand, but they do not stay down.** Smelter capacity is slow to return once idled, and the demand recovery usually arrives before the supply response.

## What it means for buyers

- **A falling LME is not a falling quote.** In April 2020 the metal fell faster than conversion and freight, so the quoted price lagged the index by two to four weeks. Negotiating on the index alone left money on the table in the wrong direction.
- **Temper and mill lead time matter more in a crash.** Mills cut shifts before they cut prices; small-lot and non-standard tempers were the first to extend lead times.
- **Contract structure is the real hedge.** Buyers who fixed only the metal price and left conversion open paid for it in 2021. Buyers who fixed both, with a tonnage band, did not.
- **Watch the inventory, not the headline.** In April 2020 LME stocks were rising while the price fell; the turn came when the destocking started, roughly three months before the price recovery was obvious in the news.

## Where it points

The 2020 low reset global cost curves and set up the 2021 energy-driven squeeze. For exporters, it also marked the beginning of a two-year period in which **quote validity shrank from 30 days to 7 days** — a practice that never went back to the old norm.
