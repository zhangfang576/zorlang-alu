On 1 January 2026 the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive period. Reporting, which had been running since October 2023, became a financial obligation: importers of aluminium into the EU must now surrender CBAM certificates covering the embedded emissions of the goods they bring in.

## How the bill is calculated

Four inputs decide the number:

1. **Embedded direct emissions** per tonne of product, based on the producing installation's actual data where available, default values otherwise.
2. **The applicable benchmark**, set against the best-performing EU installations for that product. Emissions below the benchmark reduce the obligation; emissions above it increase it.
3. **The CBAM certificate price**, linked to the EU emissions trading system allowance price.
4. **The free-allocation phase-out**, which reduces the free allowances EU producers receive over the coming years, progressively raising the effective charge on imports.

For a typical Chinese primary-aluminium-based sheet, the direct emissions intensity sits well above the EU benchmark. Using default values rather than verified actual data adds a further penalty, because defaults are deliberately conservative.

## The recycled-content arbitrage

The single most effective lever is secondary metal. Recycled aluminium carries a small fraction of the direct emissions of primary — typically around 5% or less. Where the applicable standard and the end use permit recycled content, the CBAM obligation falls close to zero even at full certificate prices.

That has created a real, defensible price difference in Europe: a low-carbon or high-recycled-content product can now command a premium that is funded by the avoided certificate cost, not by marketing.

## What the EU is doing next

The Commission has proposed extending CBAM beyond raw materials to a long list of **downstream steel- and aluminium-intensive products** from 2028 — machinery, automotive parts, appliances and similar goods. If adopted, that would move the obligation from the mill to the finished-goods importer, and cascade the carbon question down the entire bill of materials.

## What it means for buyers and suppliers

- **Ask for verified actual emissions now.** Default values are the most expensive option available. A supplier who can provide installation-level electricity and fuel data, third-party verified, is worth a premium.
- **Treat carbon as a third price line.** Metal, conversion, carbon. Quotes that fold carbon into "duty and taxes" hide the component most likely to change.
- **Specify recycled content where the standard allows.** It is the cheapest route to compliance and increasingly the deciding factor in European tenders.
- **Keep the documentation chain intact.** The importer, not the exporter, faces the legal obligation — but the importer will pass the data requirement straight back to you.

## Where it points

CBAM turns carbon intensity into a trade cost, and it is the first mechanism that prices a Chinese mill's power supply into a European buyer's landed cost. For exporters, the practical consequence is that **documentation has become a product feature**. A coil with a verified emissions figure is now a different product from an identical coil without one.
