Most buyers negotiate a single number per tonne. That number is a stack of four costs, and they behave very differently. If you know which line is moving, you know whether a higher quote is a market fact or a sales decision.

The short version: on a typical 5052 H32 plate order landed in Europe, base metal is roughly two thirds of the price, conversion about a fifth, and energy plus freight make up the rest. Only base metal tracks the LME. The other three are fixed by the plant, the furnace and the shipping line — and they are where a serious supplier can actually compete.

## Line one: base metal

Base metal is the LME cash price plus a physical premium. The premium covers the cost of getting standard ingot to a specific shape, location and delivery window, and it is regional: European duty-paid premium, US Midwest premium and Asian cif premium are three different numbers on the same day.

This is the only line you can hedge, and the only line where the quote date matters. If you are comparing two suppliers on a day when the LME moved 2%, you are not comparing suppliers, you are comparing timestamps. Ask both for the same pricing date and the same premium basis.

## Line two: conversion

Conversion is what the mill charges to turn ingot into plate: casting, rolling, heat treatment, cutting, packing and yield loss. It is quoted per tonne and it does not move with the LME.

Yield loss is the part buyers underestimate. A 2,000 mm wide plate cut to 1,240 mm leaves 38% of the coil as trim unless the mill can nest other orders into it. This is why the same alloy can carry two different conversion charges for two different widths, and why a mill that runs your width regularly will beat a mill that has to break a coil for you.

Conversion is also where specification creep shows up. Tightening flatness from EN 485-2 normal to a special tolerance, or asking for 100% ultrasonic inspection, adds process time and reject risk — that is real cost, not margin.

## Line three: energy

Rolling and annealing are energy-intensive. A cold mill and an annealing furnace dominate the plant's electricity and gas bill, and in most markets industrial energy contracts reset annually.

An energy surcharge is legitimate when it is transparent: a stated €/t per MWh band, reviewed on a fixed date. It is not legitimate when it appears as an unexplained line on an invoice after the order is confirmed. Ask for the mechanism in writing before you place the order — and ask whether it applies to the whole order or only to the unwrought portion.

## Line four: freight

Freight is the line that surprised everyone between 2021 and 2024 and has been volatile since. Plate and coil are heavy and bulky: a 20-foot container carries roughly 20–24 tonnes of aluminium depending on packing and payload limits, so freight per tonne is sensitive to both rate and stowage efficiency.

Two practical points. First, coil weighs more per container than cut plate because there is less air; if your specification allows coil, you may save freight even if the piece price is higher. Second, wooden pallets and crates add weight and volume that count against the container payload — the cheapest packing quote is not always the cheapest landed cost.

## How to read a quote in practice

Ask for the four lines separately, with the pricing date and premium basis named. A supplier who can break the price down is a supplier who knows their cost base, and that is the supplier you want when the LME moves 8% in a quarter.

Two checks that cost nothing:

- Compare conversion only, on the same alloy, temper, thickness and width. Everything else is noise.
- Compare landed cost, not FOB. Freight, insurance, duty and port charges can swing the ranking between two offers.

If you are building a budget for the next quarter, price your base metal at the forward curve rather than today's cash price, and hold conversion flat. That is closer to what a mill sees than a single spot number.
