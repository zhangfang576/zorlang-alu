If the drawing comes from a European customer and reads EN AW-6082, quote 6082. If it comes from North America or a domestic structural job and reads 6061, quote 6061. Both reach **310 MPa** tensile; the real dividing lines are yield strength (275 vs 260), extrudability, and whether the chemistry survives the customer's incoming inspection.

**Bottom line:** this is not a "which is stronger" question — it is two standard systems, each with its own optimal alloy. **Large-section extrusions go to 6082; precision machining goes to 6061.**

## 1. Chemistry: copper versus manganese

Both are Al-Mg-Si alloys with overlapping magnesium and silicon ranges. The difference sits in two elements:

- **6061 carries 0.15–0.40% copper and 0.04–0.35% chromium**, with manganese normally held below 0.15%. Copper raises strength and machinability, at a small cost in corrosion resistance and welded-joint performance.
- **6082 carries 0.40–1.00% manganese and 0.70–1.30% silicon, with copper ≤0.10%.** Manganese improves resistance to hot deformation and refines grain structure, which stabilises large-section extrusion; no copper means slightly better corrosion performance after welding and in marine atmospheres.

> The difference shows up instantly on a hand-held spectrometer: 0.2% copper and 0.02% copper are two completely different readings. If the customer inspects against an EN 10204 3.1 certificate, a substitution cannot be hidden.

## 2. Mechanical properties: same tensile, 15 MPa apart on yield

In the T6 temper the gap is smaller than most buyers expect:

| Property | 6061-T6 | 6082-T6 |
|---|---|---|
| Tensile strength Rm | 310 MPa | 310 MPa |
| Yield strength Rp0.2 | 275 MPa | 260 MPa |
| Elongation A | 12% | 10% |
| Hardness | 95 HBW | 95 HBW |
| Density | 2.70 g/cm³ | 2.70 g/cm³ |
| Chemistry signature | Cu 0.15–0.40, Cr 0.04–0.35 | Mn 0.40–1.00, Cu ≤0.10 |
| Primary standards | ASTM B209 / B221 | EN 755-2 / EN 485-2 |
| Main markets | North America, Asia | Europe |

6061's extra 15 MPa of yield comes from copper in solid solution. That matters for components designed on yield, but on brackets and frames governed by stiffness or joint strength it is rarely the deciding factor.

## 3. Extrudability: 6082's home ground

For large sections, thick walls and multi-void hollow profiles, 6082 is clearly easier to run:

- **Higher extrusion speed.** No copper and higher manganese keep flow stress lower and reduce die wear.
- **Lower quench sensitivity.** On thick walls, 6061 often ends up with a skin-to-core property gradient after press quenching; 6082 has more margin.
- **Fewer surface defects.** Large 6061 sections are prone to streaking and coarse grain rings, and scrap rate climbs with section size.

This is why European window, rail and industrial frame profiles default to 6082. Chinese rail work more often uses 6005A for the same reason — large sections go to the alloy that extrudes well.

## 4. Machining and finishing: 6061's home ground

For parts that go onto a CNC, 6061 is the easier route:

- **Chips break cleanly.** Copper improves chip form, so swarf does not birdnest and surface finish stays consistent.
- **Dimensional stability.** Thin-wall precision parts, fixtures and optical bench bases are 6061 by default across the industry.
- **Even anodising colour.** 6082's higher silicon can produce a slight grey cast or streaking in some batches — always approve an anodised sample before committing cosmetic parts.

## 5. Four selection rules

1. **Follow the drawing.** 6082 on the drawing means quote 6082; 6061 means 6061. Substitution needs written approval — verbal agreement does not count.
2. **Look at the section.** Large, thick-walled or complex hollow profiles → 6082 (or 6005A); thin-wall, small sections → either, following the customer's system.
3. **Look at the process.** Machining-dominant, tight tolerance → 6061; extrusion plus welding → 6082.
4. **Look at the market.** European customers quote 6082; North American and Southeast Asian customers quote 6061. Quoting the wrong one costs credibility on the first reply.

## 6. The real cost of substitution

Suppliers often assume "close enough on properties" is close enough — until one of three things happens:

- **Chemistry inspection.** Copper and manganese differences are obvious under a spectrometer.
- **Certificate and standard reference.** If the customer requires an EN 10204 3.1 certificate, the grade must match the contract; changing the grade means changing the contract.
- **Qualification and design assumptions.** Pressure parts and structural members already verified against one grade need re-verification after a swap.

> The safer approach is not "which is cheaper today" but "which standard system is the customer working in". A few dozen dollars per tonne saved on the wrong grade is usually repaid in full at re-inspection or rework.

## 7. Conclusion

6061 and 6082 are two answers from the same alloy family under two standard systems — one tuned to machining and North American supply chains, the other to extrusion and European supply chains. Selection really takes three questions: **which standard system, which forming process, and whether incoming inspection will happen.** Answer those and the grade usually decides itself.
